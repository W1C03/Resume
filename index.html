Sparse Tensor Response Regression and Efficient Algorithms (Principal Investigator; Awarded ¥50,000 university research grant after the proposal defense)
(1) From reading papers to posing a research question

During the mid-term review of my first research project (A piece of research I conducted in my freshman year, which will be mentioned later), I came across the TVP-VAR (Time-Varying Parameter Vector Autoregression) family of models on WoS. The model is widely used in economic, financial, and neuroimaging time-series analysis, but it has a well-recognized bottleneck: the number of parameters grows quadratically with the number of variables, and conventional parameter-evolution schemes, such as rolling windows, Kalman-filter-type updates, impose a heavy computational burden while failing to adapt to differences in data structure. After reproducing related papers, I did not stop at replication. Instead, I prepared a two-page note of my own questions—including an order-of-magnitude estimate of the parameter explosion and a summary of the failure cases of existing dimension-reduction approaches—and approached my advisor with a proposal combining tensor decomposition with adaptive feature extraction. Recognizing the potential of the tensor decomposition direction, my advisor referred me to the Dean of the School of Mathematics and Statistics. The project was formally approved with a ¥50,000 university research grant following the institutional proposal defense, and I serve as the Principal Investigator.

(2) Method design: independently building the model framework

After reviewing three technical routes: TVP-VAR, variational Bayes, and tensor decomposition, I independently designed the model framework: the classical TVP-VAR serves as the backbone; a VLMC-TKEO method is introduced for feature extraction to capture nonlinear and abrupt patterns in parameter variation; TT (Tensor-Train) decomposition is applied for structured dimension reduction on the high-dimensional parameter tensor, compressing the parameter count from quadratic to linear scale; and variational inference is used for approximate posterior training, so that the distribution of parameter variation adjusts dynamically with evolving data features rather than relying on a manually specified evolution equation. During the subsequent training phase, I brought together two classmates who were genuinely interested in the project, and we held in-depth discussions that further refined the design.

(3) Experimental validation: from idea to reproducible code

I independently implemented all of the core code. For initial validation, I selected an fMRI neuroimaging dataset and proactively identified state-of-the-art competing models as baselines for a like-for-like comparison. The results show substantial improvements of my model in both goodness of fit and parameter stability.

(4) Intellectual honesty: auditing the method against theory

Rather than rushing to finalize the results when the experiments that looked good, I stepped back to examine the algorithm at the theoretical level and identified a potential flaw in the current design. Specifically, while reviewing the literature, I realized that the second core tensor in my time-varying block does not directly carry the response variables, which creates an inconsistency in the theoretical assumptions. Instead of sidestepping or concealing this issue, I shifted the focus after the mid-term review to theoretical analysis: tracing the loophole back to its underlying assumptions and re-deriving the formulation to ensure rigor. This process taught me a lasting lesson: empirical improvement is only a necessary condition; theoretical self-consistency is what makes a method sufficient.

(5) Progress

As Principal Investigator, I independently completed the core model development and all coding. The project passed its mid-term review successfully.

Diabetes Early Risk Screening: A Hybrid Framework Combining Machine Learning and Case-Based Reasoning (Project Leader)
(1) Scoping the research: deciding what is worth doing before doing it

Unlike most projects, which were driven by coursework or competitions, this project started entirely from a real-world problem, as my grandfather currently caught diabetes. I first surveyed the global diabetes epidemic and the pain points of early screening, then compared two public datasets, Pima Indians and Diabetes Health Indicators, evaluating them along three dimensions: data scale, feature dimensionality, and real clinical applicability. Only after deciding on the CDC-BRFSS-derived Diabetes Health Indicators dataset as the research foundation did I formally launch the project. This scoping phase taught me a working habit I now follow in every project: argue first whether the data can support the question, and only then decide whether the research is worth doing. This matters far more than jumping straight into modeling.

(2) Method design: a hybrid architecture to reconcile black-box performance with trustworthiness

Rather than simply stacking neural network layers, I deliberately designed a hybrid framework combining machine learning with case-based reasoning. The machine learning component mines population-level diabetes risk patterns, while case-based reasoning retrieves historically similar patients as supporting evidence for individual predictions. On top of this, I introduced dual uncertainty quantification: aleatoric uncertainty captures noise in the data, and epistemic uncertainty captures the model's own lack of knowledge. Fusing 21 demographic, behavioral, clinical, and social-health features, the system delivers an interpretable risk screening pipeline. The motivation is straightforward: in a medical setting, a model that doctors cannot understand or challenge will never be used, no matter how accurate it is.

(3) Engineering and a rigorous stance on scope

The system architecture is complete: data cleaning and balancing, the risk estimation module, and the similar-case matching module have all been developed. I explicitly scoped the system as a tool for assisted early screening rather than clinical diagnosis, and designed the validation criteria accordingly. I am currently developing a conflict-checking and reliability assessment module that verifies the consistency between model outputs and case-based evidence, and proactively identifies scenarios where the model is likely to fail. I would rather have the system say "I don't know" when it is unreliable than let it make confident mistakes.

(4) Independent ownership

As project leader, I independently completed the research design, model implementation, and the first draft of the paper.

CogUAR: A Cognition-Inspired, Uncertainty-Guided Adaptive Retrieval-Augmented Generation Framework (Independent Researcher)
(1) Sparked by curiosity, matured into method

This project best illustrates what drives my research. During the summer mini-term of 2025, I read several papers on retrieval-augmented generation purely out of curiosity and noticed that existing RAG systems generally lack metacognitive awareness: they do not know when to retrieve or how much to retrieve, which leads to two coexisting failure modes, over-retrieval that wastes compute and under-retrieval that causes hallucination. I did not stop at observing the phenomenon. Instead, I dug deeper, studying the cognitive science literature on memory and the literature on uncertainty quantification for large language models, independently distilled the research pain points, and established the direction of the project. Curiosity lights the fire; the literature and methodology turn the fire into a research agenda. This project confirmed for me what a complete approach to research looks like.

(2) Method design: translating structures from cognitive science into computational modules

The framework design is the core contribution of this project. Drawing on the Atkinson-Shiffrin memory hierarchy theory, I independently designed a three-layer cognitive memory architecture consisting of working memory, episodic memory, and semantic memory. I proposed the UTD (Uncertainty Trend Deviation) metric to detect knowledge gaps during generation before they cause errors, rather than patching mistakes after the model has already gone wrong. I built a three-granularity adaptive retrieval scheduler that adjusts retrieval depth according to query complexity, balancing hallucination suppression against retrieval cost. The essence of this design is the migration of the uncertainty quantification idea that recurs across my tensor regression and diabetes projects into an entirely new domain. I am increasingly convinced that understanding uncertainty is the problem I most want to work on in statistical and methodological research.

(3) A complete experimental loop

I independently built the entire framework and ran all experiments on a 2,000-sample subset of the HotpotQA multi-hop question answering dataset. I conducted ablation studies to verify the contribution of each component and performed a detailed breakdown of error modes. I am currently addressing two limitations of the current work: the additional computational overhead of reasoning-time retrieval, and the reliance on a single test dataset, and I am extending the validation to multiple datasets.

(4) Independent ownership

The overall system design, implementation of all metrics and code, all experiments and result analysis, and the first draft of the paper were completed independently by me.
